The 7 loose threads holding your business back

Ten minutes here, twenty there, a copy and paste nobody sees. That's where your days go.

Alexandros Reïzos 23 November 2026 4 min read

In my last post I promised to look at what happens before the tools: how to spot a process that has got too complicated and find the problem worth solving. Here it is.

I'll start with something that happens to me too: hardly anyone knows what could be automated in their own work. It's not a lack of interest. It's that nobody stops to look at their processes. They just get done.

It's rarely one big problem

Almost no business process is designed. It grows. One day someone starts logging orders in a spreadsheet. Then someone needs a WhatsApp heads-up. Then someone goes on holiday and a colleague invents their own way of doing it. A year later you have a process that works, more or less, but that nobody would choose if they were starting from scratch.

The person who does it every day is usually the one who sees it least. It's ten minutes here, twenty there, a copy and paste nobody sees. On their own they look like nothing. Added up, they're whole days a year.

A few signs that a process has got too complicated:

  • You type the same information more than once. The client's name on the quote, then on the invoice, then in the tracking sheet.
  • "Only so-and-so knows how to do that." If someone is off sick and the process stops, it lives in their head, not in the business.
  • There's a day of the week you dread. Invoice Friday, report Monday.
  • You hear about mistakes from the client. A quote nobody followed up, an enquiry that never got a reply.

Five questions before you automate anything

Automating a messy process only multiplies the problem. So before connecting anything, I take a pen and paper and draw the process, answering five questions:

  1. What sets it off? An email, a form, a "yes" on WhatsApp.
  2. Where does the information come in, and how many times is it retyped? Each copy costs time, and it's another chance to get a tax number or an amount wrong.
  3. Who touches each step? People, tools and waiting. The waiting is the hardest part to see.
  4. What happens when something doesn't fit? The exception usually eats the most time, and it's almost never written down.
  5. How do you know it went well? If nobody knows, nobody notices when it fails.

If any answer is "it depends" or "only one person knows that", that's the problem worth solving. First I simplify, then I automate.

Three of the seven threads

When I run those questions with different kinds of businesses, the same processes keep coming up. I call them threads. Here are three of them.

New client and invoice, without typing data twice. A client accepts the quote and the copy and paste begins: their details into the spreadsheet or CRM, the invoice in the invoicing software, a folder in Drive, a heads-up by email. Simplified, you mark the quote as "Accepted" and the contact, the draft invoice, the folder and the first task are created on their own. You still check the invoice before it goes out. The data is typed once.

Supplier invoices that read themselves. They arrive as PDFs by email or as photos on WhatsApp, and Friday morning goes on opening them one by one and typing in supplier, net amount and VAT. The first fix isn't a tool at all: make every invoice come in through one place. After that, AI can read each invoice, pull out the details, file it and leave the entry ready for you to approve with one click. It's one of the projects I've built, and I'm still surprised by how much time it gives back.

Quotes that don't go cold. You prepare a tailored proposal, send it, and the day-to-day swallows it. Nobody writes a few days later to ask what they thought. Simplified, if a quote has gone unanswered for as many days as you choose, a follow-up draft appears in your inbox with the context of that proposal. You read it and press "Send". The tone is still yours.

Notice the pattern: in all three, a person still decides what goes out. The automation removes the copying and the remembering, not the judgement.
